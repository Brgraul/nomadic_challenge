## Summary

The energy transition needs a reliable power grid to bring electricity from wind farms, solar parks and other sources to the people who need it. Storms, heat and wildfires can disrupt that supply.

A storm has just passed through a region. Some areas have lost electricity, and the company responsible for keeping the power on needs to decide where to investigate first.

How could mobile sensors help operators find problems sooner, restore supply and keep renewable electricity flowing?

## Your toolkit

Imagine five mobile sensor packages that can sit on overhead power lines and automatically fly between locations within the grid.

- Each package can fly for up to **30 minutes**.
- After a flight, it must charge for **3 hours before its next flight**.
- It can **only charge on an energized power line** (a line that is live). A line without power cannot recharge it.

You choose what the packages measure, where they start and how they move as new information becomes available. Treat the basic flying and charging capabilities as given. Account for travel time, charging stops and the limits of what your sensors can detect. State any other assumptions you make, such as starting battery charge and flight speed.

## Your starting point

You will receive a map and dataset of a real section of the overhead power grid in Bavaria, together with six fictional reports from a recent storm. You can explore the files as a map, in a spreadsheet or in code. The package README explains how to use the files and what the data can and cannot tell you.

The reports are incomplete and may be misleading. Use them to choose where to begin, not as confirmed fault locations. All six reports are available from the start. Use clearly labelled hypothetical observations to demonstrate how your approach would adapt; no live sensor readings or mid-exercise updates will be provided.

## What could go wrong?

Possible problems include:

- Fallen trees or branches touching a line, or threatening to fall onto it.
- Damaged wires or connections, which may break or become unusually hot.
- Lines hanging too low, bringing them dangerously close to trees or the ground.
- Strong wind causing wires to swing, potentially touching each other or damaging equipment.
- Ice building up on wires, adding weight and pulling them down.
- Fire near a power line, threatening equipment and making the area unsafe.

Choose one problem, or a small combination. Decide what you would measure and how those measurements would help an operator act.

## What to deliver

Develop a focused solution that helps an operator investigate the affected area. Use the supplied dataset to make your approach concrete:

- **What would you measure?** Explain what the readings could tell you, and what they could not.
- **Where would you look first?** Show where you would initially place the five sensor packages, using the line or node IDs in the dataset.
- **What happens next?** Show how a finding, an unclear reading or a lack of evidence changes where you look. Account for the flight and charging limits.
- **When would you act?** Explain when you have enough information to recommend an inspection or repair, and when you need more evidence.

## What to present

Demonstrate your approach using a small tool, an interactive map, a sensor concept, a simulation, sketches, slides or another format that makes your reasoning clear.

Walk through one fictional example using the supplied dataset. Show what your solution helps an operator decide, explain your assumptions and limitations, and describe what you would test next. A useful, well-thought-through solution matters more than a complete application.
